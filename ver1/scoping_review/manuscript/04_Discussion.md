# Discussion

## Summary of Evidence

This scoping review mapped the existing structural MRI literature examining brain differences between smokers and non-smokers with schizophrenia spectrum disorders. Eight studies published between 2007 and 2026 met eligibility criteria, employing diverse methodologies including voxel-based morphometry and surface-based cortical analyses.

The evidence reveals several consistent patterns. First, specific brain regions—notably the hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and insula—show reduced gray matter volume or cortical thickness in smokers with schizophrenia compared to non-smokers. Second, findings in the prefrontal cortex are inconsistent, with an early study suggesting increased gray matter in smokers while subsequent larger studies predominantly report reductions. Third, the majority of studies (5 of 8) detected interactions between diagnostic status and smoking, suggesting that smoking may affect brain structure differently in schizophrenia compared to healthy populations. Fourth, limited evidence links smoking-related brain differences to greater symptom severity. Finally, and critically for the aims of this review, no study has measured psychological flexibility or related Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) constructs in relation to brain structure in this population.

## Regional Findings in Context

### Hippocampus

The consistent finding of reduced hippocampal volume in smokers with schizophrenia aligns with broader literature on nicotine's effects on this structure. The hippocampus contains high densities of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and is particularly vulnerable to neurotoxic effects. In schizophrenia, where hippocampal abnormalities are already well-documented, smoking may represent an additional insult. The hippocampus also plays a central role in memory, learning, and stress regulation—domains relevant to both psychiatric symptoms and addictive behavior.

### Anterior Cingulate Cortex and Insula

The ACC and insula findings are particularly noteworthy given these regions' roles in decision-making, interoception, and self-regulation. The ACC is critical for conflict monitoring, error detection, and cognitive control—processes that may be impaired in individuals who continue smoking despite awareness of health risks. The insula has been implicated in craving, interoceptive awareness, and the subjective experience of bodily states. Lesion studies have shown that insular damage can lead to spontaneous smoking cessation, underscoring this region's importance in maintaining addictive behavior.

Importantly, both the ACC and insula are consistently identified in functional neuroimaging studies of ACT interventions. Smallwood et al. (2016) found that ACT for opioid addiction altered activation in precisely these regions—the ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex. This regional overlap between smoking-related structural differences in schizophrenia and the functional correlates of psychological flexibility provides theoretical grounding for investigating this relationship.

### Prefrontal Cortex

The inconsistent prefrontal findings merit consideration. Tregellas et al. (2007), using a relatively small sample and early VBM methodology, reported increased gray matter in smokers—interpreted as a potential "protective" effect related to nicotine's procognitive properties. However, three subsequent studies with larger samples and more refined methods found prefrontal reductions in smokers. This pattern may reflect methodological advances, sample differences, or publication of initial anomalous findings that were not replicated. The weight of current evidence suggests that smoking is associated with reduced rather than increased prefrontal structure in schizophrenia.

## The Question of Directionality

A fundamental limitation of the cross-sectional designs employed by seven of eight included studies is the inability to determine causal direction. Three interpretations remain possible:

1. **Smoking causes brain changes**: Chronic nicotine exposure and associated toxins lead to gray matter reduction in vulnerable regions.

2. **Pre-existing brain differences predispose to smoking**: Individuals with smaller hippocampal, ACC, or insular volumes may be more vulnerable to nicotine addiction due to impaired self-regulation or heightened reward sensitivity.

3. **Shared vulnerability**: Common genetic or developmental factors contribute to both brain structural differences and smoking susceptibility.

Van Haren et al. (2010), the only longitudinal study, found that heavy smoking (>25 cigarettes per day) was associated with accelerated gray matter loss over time, providing some support for the first interpretation. However, even this design cannot fully exclude reverse causation, as progressive brain changes might lead to increased smoking.

## Diagnosis × Smoking Interactions

The finding that most studies detected interactions between diagnosis and smoking has important implications. If smoking affects brain structure differently in schizophrenia compared to healthy populations, this may reflect several mechanisms:

- **Baseline vulnerability**: Schizophrenia-related brain abnormalities may alter susceptibility to smoking-induced changes
- **Medication effects**: Antipsychotic medications may interact with nicotine's effects on brain structure
- **Differential exposure patterns**: Heavier smoking and higher nicotine dependence in schizophrenia may produce qualitatively different effects
- **Compensatory processes**: Some interactions (e.g., Ringin et al.'s finding of attenuated smoking effects in schizophrenia) might reflect complex compensatory mechanisms

Understanding these interactions could inform personalized approaches to smoking cessation in schizophrenia.

## The Psychological Flexibility Gap

The confirmation that no study has examined psychological flexibility in relation to brain structure represents a significant finding of this scoping review. This gap is notable for several reasons:

First, **theoretical coherence**: Psychological flexibility—the capacity to remain present, accept difficult experiences, and act according to values—represents a transdiagnostic process relevant to both psychiatric symptoms and addictive behavior. Lower psychological flexibility has been documented in schizophrenia and is associated with greater symptom severity and poorer functioning.

Second, **clinical efficacy**: ACT interventions targeting psychological flexibility have demonstrated efficacy for both smoking cessation (RR = 1.42 at short-term follow-up; Fan et al., 2025) and symptom improvement in schizophrenia. The existence of effective interventions targeting this construct increases the clinical relevance of understanding its neural substrates.

Third, **emerging neural correlates**: Recent functional neuroimaging research has begun to identify brain correlates of psychological flexibility. A 2025 resting-state fMRI study found that greater psychological flexibility (measured by AAQ-II) was associated with stronger anticorrelation between the default mode network and dorsal attention network. Critically, the brain regions implicated in psychological flexibility—prefrontal cortex, ACC, insula—substantially overlap with those showing structural differences in smokers with schizophrenia.

Fourth, **mechanistic potential**: The regional convergence between smoking-related structural differences and psychological flexibility's functional correlates suggests a testable hypothesis: individual differences in gray matter volume in these regions may relate to psychological flexibility, which in turn influences both smoking behavior and treatment response. If confirmed, this would identify psychological flexibility as a potential mediating mechanism and therapeutic target.

## Implications for Future Research

This scoping review identifies several priorities for future investigation:

### Primary Recommendation

A study combining structural MRI, validated measures of psychological flexibility (AAQ-II, CFQ, CompACT), and careful characterization of smoking behavior in schizophrenia is warranted. Such a study could directly test whether:
- Psychological flexibility differs between smokers and non-smokers with schizophrenia
- Brain structural measures in ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex correlate with psychological flexibility
- Psychological flexibility mediates or moderates the relationship between brain structure and smoking

### Methodological Recommendations

1. **Longitudinal designs**: Prospective studies tracking brain structure, smoking behavior, and psychological flexibility over time would clarify directionality.

2. **Standardized smoking assessment**: Consistent definitions of smoking status, biochemical verification, and detailed characterization of smoking intensity (cigarettes per day, pack-years, dependence scores) would improve comparability.

3. **Intervention studies**: Examining whether ACT interventions that improve psychological flexibility also produce structural brain changes would provide causal evidence.

4. **Multimodal imaging**: Combining structural MRI with functional connectivity measures could better characterize the relationship between gray matter differences and network-level function.

## Relationship to Prior Reviews: The Koster et al. Contributions

Two recent publications by Koster and colleagues provide important context for situating our findings within the broader literature.

### Comparison with Koster et al. (2025a) Systematic Review

Koster et al. (2025a) published a comprehensive systematic review in *Schizophrenia Bulletin* examining tobacco smoking and brain alterations in schizophrenia across both structural and functional MRI modalities. Their review included 8 structural and 14 functional studies, concluding that "structural studies show widespread independent and additive reductions in gray matter in relation to smoking and SSD."

Our scoping review converges with their structural findings but extends beyond their work in several important ways. First, by explicitly excluding DTI and focusing solely on T1-weighted gray matter morphometry, we provide a more homogeneous synthesis of structural findings. Second, our systematic assessment of psychological flexibility measures across all included studies—confirming this as an unexplored domain—represents a novel contribution not addressed by Koster et al. Third, our prioritization of within-diagnosis smoking comparisons (SZ-Smokers vs. SZ-Non-smokers) and explicit extraction of symptom severity correlations provides a more clinically-oriented synthesis focused on mechanisms within schizophrenia rather than general smoking effects.

The convergence between our independent reviews on the core structural findings—gray matter reductions in prefrontal, cingulate, insular, and hippocampal regions—strengthens confidence in these conclusions.

### The CHR-Diagnosed Schizophrenia Contrast

Koster et al. (2025b) examined smoking and gray matter volume in 542 clinical high-risk (CHR) individuals from the NAPLS-3 cohort, finding no cross-sectional or longitudinal associations over 8 months. This null finding in CHR stands in stark contrast to the consistent positive findings in diagnosed schizophrenia documented in our review.

This stage-specific pattern has important theoretical and clinical implications:

**Theoretical implications**: The absence of smoking-related structural differences in CHR, despite their presence in diagnosed schizophrenia, suggests that these changes may:
- Require longer cumulative smoking exposure to manifest
- Emerge as a consequence of the disease-smoking interaction over time
- Reflect synergistic effects of smoking with illness progression, medication, or other factors present only after diagnosis

**Clinical implications**: If smoking-related brain changes are not yet detectable in CHR, this represents a potential window for prevention. Early smoking cessation interventions in CHR individuals might prevent the structural alterations that become apparent after illness onset. This aligns with calls for targeted smoking prevention in psychosis prodrome.

**Methodological considerations**: Koster et al. (2025b) noted that their CHR sample had relatively low smoking frequency and intensity, which may have limited power to detect effects. Heavier smoking in diagnosed schizophrenia populations (often >20 cigarettes/day) may be necessary for structural effects to emerge.

This comparison across illness stages—from CHR (no effect) to diagnosed schizophrenia (consistent effects)—suggests that the smoking-brain relationship in psychosis may be state-dependent, with structural manifestations emerging at or after the transition to full illness.

## Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations.

First, the search was initiated from seed literature rather than comprehensive database searches, though formal searches are planned to ensure completeness. Second, consistent with scoping review methodology, we did not formally assess study quality or risk of bias. Third, the heterogeneity of methods across studies (VBM vs. surface-based analysis, varying scanner parameters, different smoking definitions) limits direct comparison of findings. Fourth, by excluding DTI studies, we did not capture white matter findings that may also be relevant. Fifth, one included study (Musket et al., 2026) was in press at the time of review, with full details pending.

Despite these limitations, this review provides a systematic mapping of existing evidence and clearly identifies the psychological flexibility gap as a priority for future research.

## Conclusions

This scoping review of structural MRI studies examining smoking in schizophrenia spectrum disorders identifies consistent reductions in hippocampal, anterior cingulate, and insular gray matter in smokers, with mixed findings in the prefrontal cortex. The majority of studies suggest that smoking affects brain structure differently in schizophrenia compared to healthy populations. Limited evidence links these brain differences to symptom severity.

The complete absence of psychological flexibility measures across all included studies confirms a significant research gap. Given the theoretical rationale—the regional overlap between smoking-related structural differences and psychological flexibility's functional correlates—and the clinical efficacy of ACT interventions in both schizophrenia and smoking cessation, investigating this relationship represents a promising direction. A study examining the triadic relationship among brain structure, psychological flexibility, and smoking in schizophrenia could provide mechanistic insights and identify novel therapeutic targets.
