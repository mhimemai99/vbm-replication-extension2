# Discussion

## Summary of Evidence

This scoping review systematically examined two neuroimaging literatures to identify gaps and potential convergence. The functional neuroimaging literature on psychological flexibility and ACT interventions remains sparse, with only four studies identified, but these studies consistently implicate the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, posterior cingulate cortex, and prefrontal cortex as neural correlates. The structural neuroimaging literature on smoking in schizophrenia, comprising eight studies, demonstrates consistent gray matter reductions in the hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and insula among smokers compared to non-smokers. These two literatures have developed independently, with no study examining psychological flexibility in relation to brain structure.

The central finding of this review is regional overlap between the two literatures. The anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and prefrontal cortex appear in both—functionally associated with psychological flexibility and structurally different in smokers with schizophrenia. This anatomical convergence provides empirical grounding for investigating psychological flexibility as a potential mechanism linking brain structure to smoking behavior.

## Gaps in the Literature

This review identifies two complementary gaps. First, the functional neuroimaging literature on psychological flexibility lacks any structural component. All four fMRI studies examined brain function, but none assessed whether psychological flexibility relates to gray matter volume or cortical thickness. Second, the structural neuroimaging literature on smoking in schizophrenia lacks psychological flexibility measurement. All eight sMRI studies characterized smoking status and brain structure, but none included the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II, Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire, or any related measure. These gaps are reciprocal: research on psychological flexibility has not incorporated structural imaging, while structural neuroimaging research has not incorporated psychological flexibility assessment.

## Interpretation of Regional Overlap

The overlap of the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and prefrontal cortex across literatures may not be coincidental, as these regions serve functions directly relevant to both psychological flexibility and addictive behavior.

The anterior cingulate cortex is critical for conflict monitoring, error detection, and cognitive control. Individuals who continue smoking despite awareness of health consequences may exhibit dysfunction in this region. Both Smallwood et al. (2016) and Aytur et al. (2021) reported changes in anterior cingulate cortex function following ACT interventions, and Jørgensen et al. (2015) and Qiu et al. (2024) found reduced thickness in this region among smokers with schizophrenia.

The insula is implicated in interoception, craving awareness, and emotional processing. Naqvi et al. (2007) demonstrated that damage to the insula can lead to spontaneous smoking cessation, suggesting this region plays a causal role in maintaining nicotine addiction. Lee et al. (2023) found increased insula activation and strengthened insula-inferior frontal gyrus connectivity following ACT for obsessive-compulsive disorder, with insula activity correlating with improvement in psychological flexibility. Among smokers with schizophrenia, Jørgensen et al. (2015) and Qiu et al. (2024) reported thinner insular cortex.

The prefrontal cortex supports executive function and value-based decision-making. Reduced prefrontal gray matter in smokers with schizophrenia (Schneider et al., 2014; Yokoyama et al., 2018) may impair the capacity for value-consistent action—a core component of psychological flexibility. Lee et al. (2023) found strengthened prefrontal connectivity following ACT, suggesting this region is responsive to interventions targeting psychological flexibility.

## Implications for Future Research

The convergence observed across literatures suggests a testable model. Individual differences in gray matter volume in the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and prefrontal cortex may relate to psychological flexibility as measured by standardized instruments such as the AAQ-II. Psychological flexibility, in turn, may influence smoking behavior and cessation success. If this model holds, ACT interventions targeting psychological flexibility may produce structural as well as functional brain changes, though this remains speculative given the absence of longitudinal sMRI studies examining ACT.

A study combining structural MRI with psychological flexibility measures in individuals with schizophrenia, systematically characterized by smoking status, could test whether the regional convergence observed across separate literatures reflects a genuine relationship between brain structure, psychological flexibility, and smoking. Such a study would address the identified gaps and could inform whether psychological flexibility represents a modifiable mechanism underlying smoking behavior in this population.

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The functional neuroimaging literature on psychological flexibility is sparse, comprising only four studies with sample sizes ranging from 9 to 42 participants. The populations studied varied considerably, including chronic pain, opioid addiction, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and healthy adults, limiting generalizability to schizophrenia. No fMRI study has examined psychological flexibility in schizophrenia or smoking populations. Consistent with scoping review methodology, formal quality assessment of included studies was not conducted. By excluding diffusion tensor imaging, white matter findings were not captured in this review.

For the structural literature, our search supplemented studies already identified by a recent systematic review (Koster et al., 2025), adding studies published after June 2023 to extend the evidence base. However, we adopted different exclusion criteria, focusing specifically on diagnosed schizophrenia and excluding clinical high-risk populations.

## Conclusions

This scoping review demonstrates that the functional neuroimaging literature identifies the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, posterior cingulate cortex, and prefrontal cortex as correlates of psychological flexibility and ACT intervention effects. The structural neuroimaging literature shows that smokers with schizophrenia have reduced gray matter in the hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and insula compared to non-smokers. Regional overlap exists for the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and prefrontal cortex. Critically, no study has combined psychological flexibility measures with structural MRI in any population.

This gap represents a research priority. Given that ACT interventions demonstrate efficacy for both psychosis and smoking cessation, understanding the structural neural basis of psychological flexibility could inform personalized treatment approaches and identify individuals who may benefit most from psychological flexibility interventions.
