# Discussion

## Summary of Evidence

This scoping review systematically bridged three previously unconnected literatures: behavioral research on psychological flexibility, functional neuroimaging of ACT processes, and structural neuroimaging of smoking in schizophrenia. The central finding is that brain regions showing functional correlates of psychological flexibility—the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, prefrontal cortex, and default mode network structures—are the same regions showing structural differences between smokers and non-smokers with schizophrenia. This regional convergence provides anatomical grounding for investigating psychological flexibility as a mechanism linking brain structure to smoking behavior in this population.

The behavioral literature confirms that psychological flexibility is reduced in schizophrenia and predicts smoking behavior, while ACT interventions targeting this construct show efficacy for both populations. The functional neuroimaging literature identifies specific brain regions—ACC, insula, PFC—where ACT produces changes. The structural neuroimaging literature, drawing on 8 studies published between 2007 and 2026, demonstrates that smokers with schizophrenia show reduced gray matter in precisely these regions. Yet despite this convergence, no study has examined psychological flexibility in relation to brain structure in this population, representing a significant and actionable research gap.

## The Psychological Flexibility Gap: A Research Priority

The complete absence of psychological flexibility measures across all 8 structural MRI studies represents the key finding of this scoping review. This gap is notable for several reasons.

**Theoretical coherence**: Psychological flexibility—the capacity to remain present, accept difficult experiences, and act according to values—represents a transdiagnostic process relevant to both psychiatric symptoms and addictive behavior. The high smoking rates in schizophrenia (2-3 times the general population) may reflect experiential avoidance: smoking as a means of escaping difficult internal experiences. Cognitive fusion with craving-related thoughts and disconnection from health-related values would maintain this behavior despite known consequences. These processes are precisely what ACT targets.

**Clinical efficacy**: ACT interventions have demonstrated efficacy for both smoking cessation (RR = 1.42; Fan et al., 2025) and symptom improvement in schizophrenia. The existence of effective interventions targeting psychological flexibility increases the clinical relevance of understanding its neural substrates.

**Neural correlates established**: Functional neuroimaging research has identified brain correlates of psychological flexibility in the ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex—the same regions showing structural differences in smokers with schizophrenia. This regional overlap is not coincidental; these regions are critical for conflict monitoring, interoception, and value-based decision-making—the very processes underlying psychological flexibility.

**Mechanistic potential**: The convergence across literatures suggests a testable model: individual differences in gray matter volume in ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex may relate to psychological flexibility, which in turn influences smoking behavior and treatment response. If confirmed, this would identify psychological flexibility as a potential mediating mechanism and therapeutic target.

## Regional Findings in Context

### Hippocampus

The consistent finding of reduced hippocampal volume in smokers with schizophrenia aligns with broader literature on nicotine's effects on this structure. The hippocampus contains high densities of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and is particularly vulnerable to neurotoxic effects. In schizophrenia, where hippocampal abnormalities are already well-documented, smoking may represent an additional insult. The hippocampus also plays a central role in memory, learning, and stress regulation—domains relevant to both psychiatric symptoms and addictive behavior.

### Anterior Cingulate Cortex and Insula

The ACC and insula findings are particularly notable given these regions' roles in psychological flexibility. The ACC is critical for conflict monitoring, error detection, and cognitive control—processes that may be impaired in individuals who continue smoking despite awareness of health risks. The insula has been implicated in craving, interoceptive awareness, and the subjective experience of bodily states. Lesion studies have shown that insular damage can lead to spontaneous smoking cessation (Naqvi et al., 2007), underscoring this region's importance in maintaining addictive behavior.

Importantly, both the ACC and insula are consistently identified in functional neuroimaging studies of ACT interventions. Smallwood et al. (2016) found that ACT for opioid addiction altered activation in precisely these regions. This regional overlap provides the key empirical basis for investigating psychological flexibility in relation to brain structure in smokers with schizophrenia.

### Prefrontal Cortex

The inconsistent prefrontal findings merit consideration. Tregellas et al. (2007), using a relatively small sample and early VBM methodology, reported increased gray matter in smokers—interpreted as a potential "protective" effect related to nicotine's procognitive properties. However, three subsequent studies with larger samples and more refined methods found prefrontal reductions in smokers. This pattern likely reflects methodological advances, sample differences, or non-replication of initial anomalous findings. The weight of current evidence suggests that smoking is associated with reduced rather than increased prefrontal structure in schizophrenia.

## The Question of Directionality

A fundamental limitation of the cross-sectional designs employed by seven of eight included structural studies is the inability to determine causal direction. Three interpretations remain possible:

1. **Smoking causes brain changes**: Chronic nicotine exposure and associated toxins lead to gray matter reduction in vulnerable regions.

2. **Pre-existing brain differences predispose to smoking**: Individuals with smaller hippocampal, ACC, or insular volumes may be more vulnerable to nicotine addiction due to impaired self-regulation or heightened reward sensitivity.

3. **Shared vulnerability**: Common genetic or developmental factors contribute to both brain structural differences and smoking susceptibility.

Van Haren et al. (2010), the only longitudinal study, found that heavy smoking (>25 cigarettes per day) was associated with accelerated gray matter loss over time, providing some support for the first interpretation. However, even this design cannot fully exclude reverse causation.

## Diagnosis × Smoking Interactions

The finding that most studies (5 of 8) detected interactions between diagnosis and smoking has important implications. If smoking affects brain structure differently in schizophrenia compared to healthy populations, this may reflect several mechanisms:

- **Baseline vulnerability**: Schizophrenia-related brain abnormalities may alter susceptibility to smoking-induced changes
- **Medication effects**: Antipsychotic medications may interact with nicotine's effects on brain structure
- **Differential exposure patterns**: Heavier smoking and higher nicotine dependence in schizophrenia may produce qualitatively different effects
- **Compensatory processes**: Some interactions (e.g., Ringin et al.'s finding of attenuated smoking effects in schizophrenia) might reflect complex compensatory mechanisms

Understanding these interactions could inform personalized approaches to smoking cessation in schizophrenia.

## Relationship to Prior Reviews

### Comparison with Koster et al. (2025a)

Koster et al. (2025a) published a comprehensive systematic review in *Schizophrenia Bulletin* examining tobacco smoking and brain alterations in schizophrenia across structural, functional, and neurochemical MRI modalities. Their review searched databases from inception to June 2023 and included 8 structural studies (6 gray matter, 2 DTI).

Our scoping review extends beyond Koster et al. in several respects. First, we include 2 additional gray matter studies (Qiu et al., 2024; Musket et al., 2026) published after their search period, representing a 33% increase to the gray matter evidence base. Second, we apply stricter diagnostic criteria by excluding DTI studies (different tissue type) and noting bipolar inclusion. Third, and most importantly, we systematically assess the psychological flexibility gap and establish regional convergence with functional neuroimaging—questions Koster et al. did not address.

Both reviews converge on the core structural finding: smoking is associated with gray matter reductions in prefrontal, cingulate, insular, and hippocampal regions in schizophrenia. This convergence from independent reviews strengthens confidence in these conclusions.

### The CHR-Diagnosed Schizophrenia Contrast

Koster et al. (2025b) found no smoking-brain associations in clinical high-risk (CHR) individuals from the NAPLS-3 cohort, despite consistent positive findings in diagnosed schizophrenia. This stage-specific pattern suggests that smoking-related structural changes may require longer cumulative exposure, emerge as a consequence of the disease-smoking interaction over time, or reflect synergistic effects of smoking with illness progression and medication.

Clinically, if smoking-related brain changes are not yet detectable in CHR, this represents a window for prevention. Early smoking cessation interventions in CHR individuals might prevent the structural alterations that become apparent after illness onset.

## Implications for Future Research

### Primary Recommendation

A study combining structural MRI, validated measures of psychological flexibility (AAQ-II, CFQ, CompACT), and careful characterization of smoking behavior in schizophrenia is warranted. Such a study could directly test whether:
- Psychological flexibility differs between smokers and non-smokers with schizophrenia
- Brain structural measures in ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex correlate with psychological flexibility
- Psychological flexibility mediates or moderates the relationship between brain structure and smoking
- Brain structure and/or psychological flexibility predict smoking cessation treatment response

### Methodological Recommendations

1. **Longitudinal designs**: Prospective studies tracking brain structure, smoking behavior, and psychological flexibility over time would clarify directionality.

2. **Standardized smoking assessment**: Consistent definitions of smoking status, biochemical verification, and detailed characterization of smoking intensity would improve comparability.

3. **Intervention studies**: Examining whether ACT interventions that improve psychological flexibility also produce structural brain changes would provide causal evidence.

4. **Multimodal imaging**: Combining structural MRI with functional connectivity measures could better characterize the relationship between gray matter differences and network-level function.

## Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations.

First, the behavioral and functional neuroimaging literatures were scoped narratively rather than systematically, as the primary empirical focus was the structural neuroimaging literature. Second, consistent with scoping review methodology, we did not formally assess study quality or risk of bias. Third, the heterogeneity of methods across structural studies (VBM vs. surface-based analysis, varying scanner parameters, different smoking definitions) limits direct comparison. Fourth, by excluding DTI studies, we did not capture white matter findings that may also be relevant. Fifth, one included study (Musket et al., 2026) was in press at the time of review.

Despite these limitations, this review provides the first systematic mapping of convergence across behavioral, functional, and structural literatures regarding psychological flexibility and smoking in schizophrenia.

## Conclusions

This scoping review demonstrates convergence across three previously unconnected literatures. Behavioral research establishes that psychological flexibility is reduced in schizophrenia and predicts smoking behavior. Functional neuroimaging identifies the ACC, insula, and prefrontal cortex as neural correlates of psychological flexibility. Structural neuroimaging reveals that smokers with schizophrenia show reduced gray matter in precisely these regions. Yet no study has examined psychological flexibility in relation to brain structure in this population.

This convergence, combined with the complete absence of psychological flexibility measures across all structural neuroimaging studies, identifies a clear research priority. A study examining brain structure, psychological flexibility, and smoking in schizophrenia could provide mechanistic insights into why smoking rates remain so elevated in this population and identify novel therapeutic targets. Given that ACT interventions targeting psychological flexibility are effective for both schizophrenia and smoking cessation, understanding the neural basis of this construct could inform personalized treatment approaches.
