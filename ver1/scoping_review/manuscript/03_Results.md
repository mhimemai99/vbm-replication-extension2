Five studies were excluded: two used diffusion tensor imaging only (Zhang et al., 2010; Cullen et al., 2012), and three examined clinical high-risk rather than diagnosed schizophrenia populations (Welch et al., 2010; Stone et al., 2012; Koster et al., 2025b).

## fMRI Studies on Psychological Flexibility

Four fMRI studies examined neural correlates of psychological flexibility or ACT interventions (Table 1). The combined sample across studies was 118 participants. Studies were heterogeneous in population, including patients with opioid addiction and chronic pain (Smallwood et al., 2016), women with chronic musculoskeletal pain (Aytur et al., 2021), patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder (Lee et al., 2023), and healthy adults (Cho et al., 2025). Three studies examined pre-post changes following ACT interventions, while one study used a cross-sectional correlational design. This heterogeneity limits the ability to draw firm conclusions about neural correlates of psychological flexibility.

**Table 1. Characteristics of Included fMRI Studies on Psychological Flexibility**

| Study | Sample | Design | PF Measure | Key Neural Findings |
|-------|--------|--------|------------|---------------------|
| Smallwood et al. (2016) | 16 ACT, 9 control; opioid addiction + chronic pain | RCT: 8-week ACT vs. health education | ACT intervention (no questionnaire) | Post-ACT: decreased activation in ACC (↓), insula (↓), PCC (↓), MFG (↓) during pain anticipation |
| Aytur et al. (2021) | 9 women with chronic pain | Pre-post: 4-week ACT | AAQ-II, CPAQ | Post-ACT: reduced connectivity within DMN (↓), salience network (↓), frontoparietal network (↓); ACC connectivity correlated with AAQ-II improvement |
| Lee et al. (2023) | 21 ACT, 21 waitlist; OCD patients | RCT: 8-week ACT vs. waitlist | AAQ-OC, CFQ | Post-ACT: increased insula activation (↑), increased insula-IFG connectivity (↑); left insula correlated with PF improvement (ρ = −0.35); PCC connectivity correlated with PF improvement (ρ = −0.49) |
| Cho et al. (2025) | 42 healthy adults | Cross-sectional correlation | AAQ-II | Higher PF associated with stronger DMN-DAN anticorrelation; no activation differences |

*Abbreviations: PF, psychological flexibility; ACT, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy; ACC, anterior cingulate cortex; PCC, posterior cingulate cortex; MFG, middle frontal gyrus; DMN, default mode network; DAN, dorsal attention network; IFG, inferior frontal gyrus; AAQ-II, Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II; AAQ-OC, AAQ for OCD; CPAQ, Chronic Pain Acceptance Questionnaire; CFQ, Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire. Direction: ↑ increased post-ACT or with higher PF, ↓ decreased post-ACT or with higher PF.*

Several brain regions appeared across studies, though findings were not always consistent in direction. The insula was identified in two studies with opposite patterns: Smallwood et al. (2016) found decreased insula activation following ACT, while Lee et al. (2023) found increased bilateral insula activation and strengthened insula-inferior frontal gyrus connectivity. These opposing findings may reflect differences in population (chronic pain vs. OCD) or task paradigm. Lee et al. (2023) reported that left insula activity correlated with psychological flexibility improvement (rho = −0.346, p = 0.036). The anterior cingulate cortex was identified by Smallwood et al. (2016) and Aytur et al. (2021), with the latter reporting that ACC connectivity changes correlated with AAQ-II improvement. The posterior cingulate cortex and related default mode network structures appeared in three studies, with Lee et al. (2023) finding that PCC connectivity correlated with psychological flexibility improvement (rho = −0.488, p = 0.001). At the network level, Aytur et al. (2021) found reduced connectivity within the default mode, salience, and frontoparietal networks following ACT, while Cho et al. (2025) found that greater psychological flexibility was associated with stronger anticorrelation between the default mode and dorsal attention networks.

The fMRI literature on psychological flexibility remains sparse, with only four studies identified and sample sizes ranging from 9 to 42 participants. No fMRI study has examined psychological flexibility in schizophrenia or smoking populations, and no study has examined whether psychological flexibility relates to brain structure.

## sMRI Studies on Smoking in Schizophrenia

Eight studies met inclusion criteria for the sMRI literature on smoking in schizophrenia (Table 2).

**Table 2. Characteristics of Included sMRI Studies on Smoking in Schizophrenia**

| Study | Sample | Method | Regions Examined | Key Findings in Smokers vs Non-Smokers |
|-------|--------|--------|------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Tregellas et al. (2007) | 32 SCZ smokers, 32 SCZ non-smokers | VBM | Whole brain | Larger GM volume in medial frontal gyrus (↑) and STG (↑) |
| Van Haren et al. (2010) | 159 SCZ (smoking data), 50 HC; 5-year follow-up | Automated segmentation | Whole brain | Heavy smokers: accelerated GM loss over time in frontal, temporal, parietal regions |
| Schneider et al. (2014) | 54 SCZ smokers, 135 SCZ non-smokers | FreeSurfer | Cortical thickness, subcortical volumes | Thinner DLPFC (↓), smaller hippocampus (↓ 2.2%), smaller amygdala (↓) |
| Jørgensen et al. (2015) | 523 SCZ/BD smokers, 220 non-smokers | FreeSurfer | Cortical thickness | Thinner ACC (↓), thinner insula (↓), thinner lateral OFC (↓) |
| Yokoyama et al. (2018) | 48 SCZ smokers, 52 SCZ non-smokers | VBM | Whole brain | Reduced GM in left middle frontal gyrus (↓), left precentral gyrus (↓) |
| Ringin et al. (2022) | 269 SCZ smokers, 265 SCZ non-smokers | FreeSurfer | Subcortical volumes | No main effect of smoking; diagnosis × smoking interaction for thalamus and pallidum |
| Qiu et al. (2024) | Multi-site: 1,262 SCZ total | Multimodal (VBM, CT) | Whole brain | Reduced GM in hippocampus (↓), ACC (↓), insula (↓), temporal cortex (↓) |
| Musket et al. (2026) | 253 SCZ smokers, 253 SCZ non-smokers | FreeSurfer | Hippocampus | Smaller hippocampal volume (↓), dose-response with pack-years |

*Abbreviations: SCZ, schizophrenia; HC, healthy controls; BD, bipolar disorder; GM, gray matter; VBM, voxel-based morphometry; CT, cortical thickness; DLPFC, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex; ACC, anterior cingulate cortex; OFC, orbitofrontal cortex; STG, superior temporal gyrus. Direction: ↑ increased in smokers, ↓ reduced in smokers.*

The hippocampus emerged as the most consistently implicated region, with reductions in smokers reported by Schneider et al. (2014; 2.2% volume reduction), Qiu et al. (2024), and Musket et al. (2026). Musket et al. (2026) further demonstrated a dose-response relationship, with greater hippocampal volume reduction associated with higher pack-years of smoking. The anterior cingulate cortex showed reduced thickness in smokers in two large studies (Jørgensen et al., 2015, N = 743; Qiu et al., 2024, N = 1,262). The insula similarly showed reduced thickness in smokers (Jørgensen et al., 2015; Qiu et al., 2024).

Findings for the prefrontal cortex were mixed and may depend on the specific subregion examined. Tregellas et al. (2007) reported increased gray matter volume in the medial frontal gyrus in smokers, whereas Schneider et al. (2014) found reduced DLPFC thickness and Yokoyama et al. (2018) found reduced left middle frontal gyrus volume. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences (VBM vs. FreeSurfer), sample characteristics, or true regional heterogeneity within prefrontal cortex.

Van Haren et al. (2010), the only longitudinal study, found that heavy smoking was associated with accelerated gray matter loss over a 5-year follow-up period, suggesting smoking may contribute to progressive brain changes rather than reflecting only pre-existing differences. Ringin et al. (2022) found no main effect of smoking on subcortical volumes but reported significant diagnosis × smoking interactions for thalamus and pallidum, indicating that smoking effects on these structures may differ between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy controls.

## Psychological Flexibility Measures in sMRI Studies

A key aim of this review was to determine whether any sMRI study of smoking in schizophrenia had measured psychological flexibility. None of the eight included studies measured psychological flexibility using the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II, Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire, Comprehensive Assessment of ACT Processes, or any related ACT construct. This absence represents a gap in the literature: the relationship between brain structure and psychological flexibility remains unexamined in any population.

## Regional Overlap Across Literatures

Several brain regions appeared in both the fMRI literature on psychological flexibility and the sMRI literature on smoking in schizophrenia (Table 3). The anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and prefrontal cortex were identified in both literatures. However, this overlap should be interpreted cautiously. These regions are among the most frequently reported in neuroimaging research generally, appearing across diverse psychiatric conditions and cognitive processes. Whether the overlap reflects a meaningful relationship specific to psychological flexibility and smoking, or simply the ubiquity of these regions in neuroimaging findings, cannot be determined from cross-literature comparison alone.
