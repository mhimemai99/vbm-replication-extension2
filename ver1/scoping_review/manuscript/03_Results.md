# Results

## Overview

This scoping review examined three literatures: (1) behavioral evidence on psychological flexibility in schizophrenia and smoking/addiction, (2) functional neuroimaging of psychological flexibility and ACT interventions, and (3) structural neuroimaging of smoking in schizophrenia spectrum disorders. Results from each literature are presented below, followed by a convergence analysis comparing brain regions across literatures.

---

## Literature 1: Behavioral Evidence on Psychological Flexibility

### Psychological Flexibility in Schizophrenia

Multiple studies have documented lower psychological flexibility in individuals with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls. Using the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II), individuals with schizophrenia show significantly higher scores (indicating greater psychological inflexibility) than non-clinical populations. This inflexibility—characterized by experiential avoidance, cognitive fusion, and disconnection from values—is associated with greater psychiatric symptom severity, including both positive and negative symptoms.

Psychological inflexibility has been conceptualized as a transdiagnostic vulnerability factor that maintains psychiatric symptoms through a cycle of avoidance. In schizophrenia specifically, experiential avoidance may lead individuals to avoid distressing internal experiences (such as intrusive thoughts or unusual perceptions), paradoxically increasing their frequency and intensity. Cognitive fusion—the tendency to treat thoughts as literal truths—may exacerbate symptoms by preventing psychological distance from distressing cognitions.

### Psychological Flexibility and Smoking/Addiction

A parallel literature has established psychological flexibility as a mechanism underlying addictive behavior. Smokers demonstrate lower psychological flexibility than non-smokers, and psychological inflexibility predicts smoking maintenance and relapse. The relationship operates through multiple pathways: experiential avoidance drives smoking as a means of escaping negative internal states; cognitive fusion with craving-related thoughts reduces capacity to resist urges; and disconnection from values prevents motivation for change even when health consequences are known.

In individuals with dual diagnosis (psychiatric disorder plus substance use), these mechanisms may be particularly pronounced. The high smoking rates observed in schizophrenia (2-3 times the general population) may reflect attempts to regulate difficult internal experiences through nicotine's anxiolytic and cognitive effects—a form of experiential avoidance that perpetuates both the psychiatric symptoms and the addiction.

### ACT Intervention Efficacy

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), which directly targets psychological flexibility, has demonstrated efficacy for both schizophrenia and smoking cessation. A recent meta-analysis (Fan et al., 2025) found that ACT interventions for smoking cessation achieved a risk ratio of 1.42 at short-term follow-up compared to control conditions, indicating substantially higher quit rates.

In schizophrenia, ACT interventions have shown efficacy for reducing symptom distress, improving functioning, and enhancing quality of life. The therapeutic mechanism involves increasing psychological flexibility—specifically, promoting acceptance of difficult experiences, defusion from unhelpful thoughts, and engagement in value-consistent action despite symptoms.

The dual efficacy of ACT for both schizophrenia and smoking suggests that psychological flexibility represents a common mechanism underlying both conditions—a transdiagnostic process that, if modified, could simultaneously address psychiatric symptoms and addictive behavior.

## Literature 2: Functional Neuroimaging of Psychological Flexibility

### Neural Correlates of ACT Interventions

Functional neuroimaging studies have begun to identify brain regions showing altered activation following ACT interventions. Smallwood et al. (2016) examined ACT for opioid addiction and chronic pain using fMRI, finding that ACT treatment was associated with changes in activation in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula, and prefrontal cortex—regions critical for self-regulation, interoception, and decision-making.

These findings align with the theoretical model underlying ACT: the ACC is implicated in conflict monitoring and cognitive control, the insula in interoceptive awareness and craving, and the prefrontal cortex in executive function and value-based decision-making. ACT interventions may produce their effects by modulating activity in this network, enabling individuals to observe internal experiences without automatic reactivity.

### Resting-State Connectivity and Psychological Flexibility

Recent research has examined resting-state functional connectivity correlates of psychological flexibility. A 2025 study found that greater psychological flexibility (lower AAQ-II scores) was associated with stronger anticorrelation between the default mode network (DMN) and dorsal attention network (DAN). This pattern suggests that psychologically flexible individuals show clearer differentiation between internally-directed (DMN) and externally-directed (DAN) brain states—consistent with the capacity to be present and responsive to current context rather than entangled in ruminative thought.

Additional studies have implicated the frontoparietal control network in psychological flexibility, consistent with the role of cognitive control in flexibly adapting behavior based on context and values rather than habitual patterns.

### Key Brain Regions Identified

Across the functional neuroimaging literature, the following brain regions consistently emerge as correlates of psychological flexibility and ACT processes:

**Table 1. Brain Regions Implicated in Psychological Flexibility (fMRI Literature)**

| Brain Region | Functional Role | Psychological Flexibility Correlate |
|--------------|-----------------|-------------------------------------|
| Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) | Conflict monitoring, cognitive control | ACT intervention changes (Smallwood et al.) |
| Insula | Interoception, craving, emotional awareness | ACT intervention changes (Smallwood et al.) |
| Prefrontal Cortex (PFC) | Executive function, value-based decision-making | ACT intervention changes; frontoparietal connectivity |
| Default Mode Network (DMN) | Self-referential thought, rumination | DMN-DAN anticorrelation with flexibility |

## Literature 3: Structural Neuroimaging of Smoking in Schizophrenia

### Study Selection

The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1) illustrates the study selection process. From seed literature and reference screening of a recent systematic review (Koster et al., 2025a), 13 potentially relevant studies were identified. After full-text assessment against eligibility criteria, 8 studies were included in the final synthesis.

Five studies were excluded for the following reasons:
- **Wrong imaging modality (n=2)**: Zhang et al. (2010) and Cullen et al. (2012) used diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to examine white matter microstructure rather than T1-weighted structural MRI for gray matter morphometry.
- **Wrong population (n=3)**: Welch et al. (2010), Stone et al. (2012), and Koster et al. (2025b) examined clinical high-risk (CHR) or at-risk mental state (ARMS) populations rather than individuals with diagnosed schizophrenia spectrum disorders.

### Synthesis of Results by Brain Region

Findings are organized by anatomical region, focusing on within-diagnosis smoking comparisons (SZ-Smokers vs. SZ-Non-smokers) as the primary outcome.

#### Prefrontal Cortex

Four studies examined prefrontal cortex structure in relation to smoking status, with mixed findings regarding direction of effect.

Tregellas et al. (2007) reported that smokers with schizophrenia had **greater** gray matter volume in lateral prefrontal regions compared to non-smokers with schizophrenia, suggesting a potential "protective" effect of smoking. However, three subsequent studies reported the opposite pattern. Schneider et al. (2014) found that smokers with schizophrenia had approximately 2.5% **lower** dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) volume compared to non-smokers. Yokoyama et al. (2018) demonstrated additive effects of schizophrenia and smoking on left prefrontal gray matter reduction, with pack-years negatively correlated with volume. Qiu et al. (2024) reported positive correlations between tobacco use and prefrontal gray matter volume in their multimodal analysis, though interpretation is complicated by the fusion methodology.

The inconsistency between Tregellas et al.'s findings and later studies may reflect differences in sample size (early study had smaller N), analytical approach, or cohort characteristics.

#### Hippocampus

Three studies examined hippocampal structure, with consistent findings of reduced volume in smokers.

Schneider et al. (2014) reported that smokers with schizophrenia had approximately 2.2% **lower** hippocampal volume compared to non-smokers with schizophrenia. Qiu et al. (2024) found negative correlations between tobacco use and hippocampal gray matter volume. Musket et al. (2026) focused specifically on hippocampal structure in schizophrenia with comorbid substance use disorder, with detailed findings pending full extraction.

**Summary**: Hippocampal volume reduction in smokers with schizophrenia was consistently observed across studies.

#### Anterior Cingulate Cortex

Two studies examined the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), both finding reduced structure in smokers.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) reported that smokers with severe mental illness (including schizophrenia) had thinner left rostral ACC compared to non-smokers, with an effect size of approximately 0.05mm. Qiu et al. (2024) identified ACC abnormalities as part of default mode network alterations associated with tobacco use.

**Summary**: ACC showed consistent reductions in smokers across studies.

#### Insula

Two studies examined insular cortex, both finding reduced thickness or volume in smokers.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) found thinner left insula in smokers with severe mental illness. Qiu et al. (2024) reported negative correlations between tobacco use and insular gray matter volume.

**Summary**: Insula showed consistent reductions in smokers across studies.

#### Temporal Cortex

Only Tregellas et al. (2007) specifically examined temporal regions, reporting **greater** right superior temporal gyrus (STG) gray matter in smokers with schizophrenia compared to non-smokers. This finding has not been replicated in subsequent studies.

#### Global Brain Measures

Van Haren et al. (2010) conducted the only longitudinal study, examining progressive brain changes over time. Smoking status did not explain progressive gray matter loss in the overall sample. However, heavy smoking (>25 cigarettes per day) was associated with accelerated gray matter loss over the follow-up period.

Ringin et al. (2022) examined global and regional measures in a large sample, finding that healthy control smokers showed widespread cortical reductions, whereas schizophrenia spectrum disorder smokers showed an attenuated smoking effect, suggesting a diagnosis × smoking interaction.

### Diagnosis × Smoking Interactions

Five of eight studies (63%) reported some form of interaction between diagnostic status and smoking on brain structure, though the direction and interpretation varied.

**Table 3. Diagnosis × Smoking Interactions**

| Study | Interaction | Interpretation |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| Tregellas et al. (2007) | Yes | Smoking appeared to moderate gray matter deficits in schizophrenia |
| Van Haren et al. (2010) | No | No significant interaction detected |
| Schneider et al. (2014) | Partial | Non-smoker SZ ≈ HC volumes; smoker SZ < HC |
| Jørgensen et al. (2015) | Yes | Smoking effects were specific to patients |
| Yokoyama et al. (2018) | No | Additive rather than interactive effects |
| Ringin et al. (2022) | Yes | SSD showed attenuated smoking effect compared to HC |
| Qiu et al. (2024) | Yes | Tobacco × schizophrenia interaction in PFC-symptom correlations |
| Musket et al. (2026) | No | No explicit interaction reported |

The pattern of interactions suggests that the relationship between smoking and brain structure may differ between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy populations, though the specific nature of this difference remains unclear.

### Symptom Severity Correlations

Five studies assessed psychiatric symptoms using standardized measures (primarily PANSS), but only two explicitly examined the relationship between smoking, brain structure, and symptom severity.

Yokoyama et al. (2018) found that smaller left prefrontal cortex volume was associated with greater positive and negative symptom severity. Furthermore, pack-years of smoking correlated with both reduced prefrontal volume and increased symptoms, suggesting a potential mediating pathway.

Qiu et al. (2024) reported that prefrontal gray matter volume was negatively correlated with symptom burden specifically in smokers with schizophrenia, providing additional evidence linking smoking-related brain changes to clinical outcomes.

**Summary**: Limited but consistent evidence suggests that smoking-related brain structural differences may be associated with greater symptom severity.

### Comparison with Koster et al. Reviews

Two recent publications by Koster and colleagues provide important context for interpreting our findings.

#### Koster et al. 2025a (Schizophrenia Bulletin) - Systematic Review

Koster et al. (2025a) published a systematic review examining the association between chronic tobacco smoking and brain alterations in schizophrenia, including structural, functional, and neurochemical MRI studies. Their review searched databases from inception to June 7, 2023 (with reference tracking to February 2024) and included 8 structural studies (6 gray matter + 2 DTI) and 14 functional studies.

**Table 5. Comparison with Koster et al. (2025a) Systematic Review**

| Feature | Present Scoping Review | Koster et al. (2025a) |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Focus** | T1-weighted structural MRI only | Structural + Functional + Neurochemical MRI |
| **Population criteria** | SZ, schizoaffective, schizophreniform | SSD (SZ, SZA, SZF, psychotic disorder NOS) |
| **Bipolar included** | No | Yes (Jørgensen study included BP patients) |
| **Gray matter studies** | 8 | 6 |
| **DTI studies** | 0 (excluded) | 2 (Cullen, Zhang) |
| **Search end date** | 2026 (includes Qiu 2024, Musket 2026) | June 2023 |
| **Psychological flexibility** | Explicitly assessed (gap confirmed) | Not assessed |

**Overlapping gray matter studies (6):** Tregellas 2007, Van Haren 2010, Schneider 2014, Jørgensen 2015, Yokoyama 2018, Ringin 2022.

**Studies unique to our review (2):** Qiu et al. (2024) and Musket et al. (2026), representing a 33% increase to the gray matter evidence base beyond Koster et al.'s search period.

Both reviews converge on the conclusion that smoking is associated with gray matter reductions in schizophrenia, particularly in prefrontal, cingulate, insular, and hippocampal regions.

#### Koster et al. 2025b - CHR Study

Koster et al. (2025b) conducted a longitudinal study examining smoking and gray matter volume in 542 individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR-P) from the NAPLS-3 cohort. Notably, they found **no cross-sectional or longitudinal associations** between smoking and gray matter volume over 8 months in CHR individuals.

This contrast is clinically significant: smoking-related gray matter differences appear detectable in diagnosed schizophrenia but not yet evident in the CHR stage, suggesting effects may emerge after diagnosis or with prolonged exposure.

## Convergence Analysis

### Regional Overlap Across Literatures

The central aim of this scoping review was to determine whether brain regions implicated in psychological flexibility (from functional neuroimaging) overlap with regions showing structural differences between smokers and non-smokers with schizophrenia (from structural neuroimaging). Table 6 presents this convergence analysis.

**Table 6. Convergence of Brain Regions Across Literatures**

| Brain Region | Functional Neuroimaging (Psychological Flexibility) | Structural Neuroimaging (Smoking in SZ) | Convergence |
|--------------|-----------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------|
| **Anterior Cingulate Cortex** | ACT intervention changes (Smallwood et al.); cognitive control | Reduced thickness in smokers (Jørgensen; Qiu) | **YES** |
| **Insula** | ACT intervention changes; interoception/craving | Reduced thickness in smokers (Jørgensen; Qiu) | **YES** |
| **Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC)** | ACT intervention changes; frontoparietal connectivity | Reduced volume in smokers (Schneider; Yokoyama) | **YES** |
| **Default Mode Network/Hippocampus** | DMN-DAN anticorrelation with flexibility | Reduced hippocampal volume (Schneider; Qiu) | **YES** |
| **Striatum/Putamen** | Lower flexibility → altered connectivity | Reduced volume (Qiu) | **YES** |

All major regions showing functional correlates of psychological flexibility also show structural differences in smokers with schizophrenia. This regional convergence is notable and provides anatomical grounding for the hypothesis that psychological flexibility may relate to smoking behavior through brain structure.

### Interpretation of Convergence

The convergence of regions across functional and structural literatures suggests a testable model:

1. **Psychological flexibility** is associated with functional patterns in ACC, insula, PFC, and DMN connectivity
2. **Smoking in schizophrenia** is associated with reduced gray matter in these same regions
3. **Hypothesis**: Individual differences in gray matter volume in these regions may relate to psychological flexibility, which in turn influences smoking behavior and treatment response

This model remains entirely untested, as no study has examined all three components (brain structure, psychological flexibility, smoking) together in schizophrenia.

---

## Summary of Key Findings

1. **Behavioral evidence**: Psychological flexibility is reduced in schizophrenia and predicts smoking behavior. ACT interventions targeting psychological flexibility show efficacy for both populations.

2. **Functional neuroimaging evidence**: Psychological flexibility and ACT interventions are associated with functional changes in ACC, insula, prefrontal cortex, and DMN connectivity.

3. **Structural neuroimaging evidence**: Smoking in schizophrenia is associated with reduced gray matter in hippocampus, ACC, and insula, with mixed findings in prefrontal cortex. Diagnosis × smoking interactions suggest differential effects in schizophrenia versus healthy populations.

4. **Convergence confirmed**: All major regions showing functional correlates of psychological flexibility also show structural differences in smokers with schizophrenia.

5. **Critical gap identified**: No structural neuroimaging study has measured psychological flexibility, leaving the relationship between brain structure, psychological flexibility, and smoking in schizophrenia entirely unexplored.

6. **Stage-specific pattern**: Unlike diagnosed schizophrenia, CHR individuals show no smoking-brain associations (Koster et al., 2025b), suggesting structural changes may emerge after illness onset.
