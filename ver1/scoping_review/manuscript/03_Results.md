# Results

## Study Selection

The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1) illustrates the study selection process. From seed literature and reference screening of a recent systematic review (Koster et al., 2025), 13 potentially relevant studies were identified. After full-text assessment against eligibility criteria, 8 studies were included in the final synthesis.

Five studies were excluded for the following reasons:
- **Wrong imaging modality (n=2)**: Zhang et al. (2010) and Cullen et al. (2012) used diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to examine white matter microstructure rather than T1-weighted structural MRI for gray matter morphometry.
- **Wrong population (n=3)**: Welch et al. (2010), Stone et al. (2012), and Koster et al. (2025) examined clinical high-risk (CHR) or at-risk mental state (ARMS) populations rather than individuals with diagnosed schizophrenia spectrum disorders.

## Synthesis of Results by Brain Region

Findings are organized by anatomical region, focusing on within-diagnosis smoking comparisons (SZ-Smokers vs. SZ-Non-smokers) as the primary outcome.

### Prefrontal Cortex

Four studies examined prefrontal cortex structure in relation to smoking status, with mixed findings regarding direction of effect.

Tregellas et al. (2007) reported that smokers with schizophrenia had **greater** gray matter volume in lateral prefrontal regions compared to non-smokers with schizophrenia, suggesting a potential "protective" effect of smoking. However, three subsequent studies reported the opposite pattern. Schneider et al. (2014) found that smokers with schizophrenia had approximately 2.5% **lower** dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) volume compared to non-smokers. Yokoyama et al. (2018) demonstrated additive effects of schizophrenia and smoking on left prefrontal gray matter reduction, with pack-years negatively correlated with volume. Qiu et al. (2024) reported positive correlations between tobacco use and prefrontal gray matter volume in their multimodal analysis, though interpretation is complicated by the fusion methodology.

The inconsistency between Tregellas et al.'s findings and later studies may reflect differences in sample size (early study had smaller N), analytical approach, or cohort characteristics.

### Hippocampus

Three studies examined hippocampal structure, with consistent findings of reduced volume in smokers.

Schneider et al. (2014) reported that smokers with schizophrenia had approximately 2.2% **lower** hippocampal volume compared to non-smokers with schizophrenia. Qiu et al. (2024) found negative correlations between tobacco use and hippocampal gray matter volume. Musket et al. (2026) focused specifically on hippocampal structure in schizophrenia with comorbid substance use disorder, with detailed findings pending full extraction.

**Summary**: Hippocampal volume reduction in smokers with schizophrenia was consistently observed across studies.

### Anterior Cingulate Cortex

Two studies examined the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), both finding reduced structure in smokers.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) reported that smokers with severe mental illness (including schizophrenia) had thinner left rostral ACC compared to non-smokers, with an effect size of approximately 0.05mm. Qiu et al. (2024) identified ACC abnormalities as part of default mode network alterations associated with tobacco use.

**Summary**: ACC showed consistent reductions in smokers across studies.

### Insula

Two studies examined insular cortex, both finding reduced thickness or volume in smokers.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) found thinner left insula in smokers with severe mental illness. Qiu et al. (2024) reported negative correlations between tobacco use and insular gray matter volume.

**Summary**: Insula showed consistent reductions in smokers across studies.

### Temporal Cortex

Only Tregellas et al. (2007) specifically examined temporal regions, reporting **greater** right superior temporal gyrus (STG) gray matter in smokers with schizophrenia compared to non-smokers. This finding has not been replicated in subsequent studies.

### Global Brain Measures

Van Haren et al. (2010) conducted the only longitudinal study, examining progressive brain changes over time. Smoking status did not explain progressive gray matter loss in the overall sample. However, heavy smoking (>25 cigarettes per day) was associated with accelerated gray matter loss over the follow-up period.

Ringin et al. (2022) examined global and regional measures in a large sample, finding that healthy control smokers showed widespread cortical reductions, whereas schizophrenia spectrum disorder smokers showed an attenuated smoking effect, suggesting a diagnosis × smoking interaction.

## Diagnosis × Smoking Interactions

Five of eight studies (63%) reported some form of interaction between diagnostic status and smoking on brain structure, though the direction and interpretation varied.

**Table 2. Diagnosis × Smoking Interactions**

| Study | Interaction | Interpretation |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| Tregellas et al. (2007) | Yes | Smoking appeared to moderate gray matter deficits in schizophrenia |
| Van Haren et al. (2010) | No | No significant interaction detected |
| Schneider et al. (2014) | Partial | Non-smoker SZ ≈ HC volumes; smoker SZ < HC |
| Jørgensen et al. (2015) | Yes | Smoking effects were specific to patients |
| Yokoyama et al. (2018) | No | Additive rather than interactive effects |
| Ringin et al. (2022) | Yes | SSD showed attenuated smoking effect compared to HC |
| Qiu et al. (2024) | Yes | Tobacco × schizophrenia interaction in PFC-symptom correlations |
| Musket et al. (2026) | No | No explicit interaction reported |

The pattern of interactions suggests that the relationship between smoking and brain structure may differ between individuals with schizophrenia and healthy populations, though the specific nature of this difference remains unclear.

## Symptom Severity Correlations

Five studies assessed psychiatric symptoms using standardized measures (primarily PANSS), but only two explicitly examined the relationship between smoking, brain structure, and symptom severity.

Yokoyama et al. (2018) found that smaller left prefrontal cortex volume was associated with greater positive and negative symptom severity. Furthermore, pack-years of smoking correlated with both reduced prefrontal volume and increased symptoms, suggesting a potential mediating pathway.

Qiu et al. (2024) reported that prefrontal gray matter volume was negatively correlated with symptom burden specifically in smokers with schizophrenia, providing additional evidence linking smoking-related brain changes to clinical outcomes.

**Summary**: Limited but consistent evidence suggests that smoking-related brain structural differences may be associated with greater symptom severity.

## Comparison with Related Reviews

Two recent publications by Koster and colleagues provide important context for interpreting our findings.

### Koster et al. 2025 (Schizophrenia Bulletin) - Systematic Review

Koster et al. (2025a) published a systematic review examining the association between chronic tobacco smoking and brain alterations in schizophrenia, including structural, functional, and neurochemical MRI studies. Their review searched databases from inception to June 7, 2023 (with reference tracking to February 2024) and included 8 structural studies (6 gray matter + 2 DTI) and 14 functional studies.

**Table 4. Detailed Comparison with Koster et al. (2025a) Systematic Review**

| Feature | Present Scoping Review | Koster et al. (2025a) |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Focus** | T1-weighted structural MRI only | Structural + Functional + Neurochemical MRI |
| **Population criteria** | SZ, schizoaffective, schizophreniform | SSD (SZ, SZA, SZF, psychotic disorder NOS) |
| **Bipolar included** | **No** | **Yes** (Jørgensen study included BP patients) |
| **Gray matter studies** | **8** | **6** |
| **DTI studies** | **0** (excluded) | **2** (Cullen, Zhang) |
| **Search end date** | 2024 (includes Qiu 2024, Musket 2026) | June 2023 |
| **Psychological flexibility** | Explicitly assessed (gap confirmed) | Not assessed |
| **Symptom correlations** | Explicitly extracted | Not primary focus |

**Overlapping gray matter studies (6):** Tregellas 2007, Van Haren 2010, Schneider 2014, Jørgensen 2015, Yokoyama 2018, Ringin 2022.

**Studies unique to our review (2):** Qiu et al. (2024) and Musket et al. (2026) were published after Koster et al.'s search period.

**Studies in Koster but excluded from ours (2):** Cullen et al. (DTI) and Zhang et al. (DTI) were excluded from our review as DTI measures white matter microstructure rather than gray matter morphometry.

Both reviews converge on the conclusion that smoking is associated with gray matter reductions in schizophrenia, particularly in prefrontal, cingulate, insular, and hippocampal regions. However, our scoping review extends beyond Koster et al. by:
1. Focusing exclusively on gray matter morphometry (excluding DTI)
2. Including two additional recent studies (Qiu 2024, Musket 2026)
3. Applying stricter diagnostic criteria (excluding studies with bipolar patients)
4. Systematically assessing the psychological flexibility gap
5. Prioritizing within-diagnosis smoking comparisons over general smoking effects
6. Extracting symptom severity correlations as a secondary outcome

### Koster et al. 2025 (Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science) - CHR Study

Koster et al. (2025b) conducted a longitudinal study examining smoking and gray matter volume in 542 individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR-P) from the NAPLS-3 cohort. Notably, they found **no cross-sectional or longitudinal associations** between smoking and gray matter volume over 8 months in CHR individuals.

**Table 5. Comparison: Diagnosed Schizophrenia vs. Clinical High Risk**

| Feature | Present Review (Diagnosed SZ) | Koster et al. 2025b (CHR-P) |
|---------|------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Population** | Diagnosed schizophrenia | Clinical high-risk |
| **Smoking-GMV association** | Yes (consistent reductions) | No association detected |
| **Regions affected** | Hippocampus, ACC, insula, PFC | None significant |
| **Interpretation** | Smoking associated with structural differences | Effects may emerge after diagnosis/with prolonged exposure |

This contrast is clinically significant: smoking-related gray matter differences appear to be detectable in diagnosed schizophrenia but not yet evident in the CHR stage. This suggests either:
- Smoking effects require longer exposure or more severe illness to manifest structurally
- The CHR sample had lower smoking intensity (noted as a limitation in their study)
- Structural changes emerge as a consequence of the disease-smoking interaction over time

This stage-specific pattern has implications for prevention: if smoking-related brain changes are not yet present in CHR, early smoking cessation interventions might prevent these structural alterations.

## Summary of Key Findings

1. **Brain regions consistently affected**: Hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and insula showed consistent reductions in smokers with schizophrenia across multiple studies.

2. **Mixed findings in prefrontal cortex**: The earliest study suggested increased gray matter in smokers, while subsequent studies predominantly found reductions. Methodological differences may account for this inconsistency.

3. **Diagnosis × smoking interactions**: The majority of studies (5/8) found some interaction effect, suggesting that smoking may affect brain structure differently in schizophrenia compared to healthy populations.

4. **Symptom correlations**: Limited evidence suggests smoking-related brain differences are associated with greater symptom severity.

5. **Psychological flexibility gap**: No study has examined psychological flexibility in relation to brain structure in this population, representing a clear research gap and opportunity for future investigation.

6. **Contrast with CHR populations**: Unlike diagnosed schizophrenia where smoking-GMV associations are consistently observed, CHR individuals show no such associations (Koster et al., 2025b), suggesting smoking-related structural changes may emerge after illness onset or with prolonged exposure.
